## Digital Objects & Representations

Digital objects in esports earnings and competitive gaming include tournament records, prize pool listings, player earnings tables, match statistics, and rankings. These objects represent competitive events that occur digitally and are recorded through online systems. They transform live gameplay and competitions into stable representations that can be searched, compared, and archived. Esports earnings data, in particular, represents how value, labor, and success are quantified within gaming culture.

---

## Digitization Processes

Most digital objects in esports are **not digitized from physical materials**, but instead created through digital processes from the start. Tournament organizers, game publishers, and platforms generate records through online registration systems, automated match tracking, and digital payment infrastructures. Community editors and third-party platforms then aggregate and standardize this information.

This differs from digitization processes observed in HathiTrust, where physical texts are scanned and processed using OCR to extract readable text. While esports data avoids OCR errors, it faces challenges such as inconsistent reporting, missing events, and reliance on community verification rather than formal institutional standards.

---

## Historical Equivalents

Historical equivalents to esports earnings records include printed sports almanacs, newspaper sports pages, tournament programs, and financial ledgers documenting athletic competitions. These materials recorded winners, prize money, and event outcomes, but were limited by geography and publication access.

Digital esports records differ by being globally accessible, frequently updated, and centralized. However, like historical records, they reflect power and visibility, as more popular games and leagues tend to be documented more thoroughly than smaller or regional competitions.

---

## Born-Digital Materials

Esports earnings and competitive gaming records are primarily **born-digital materials**. Match results, prize pools, livestreams, and player statistics are created digitally and never exist in physical form. Unlike digitized print materials, born-digital esports objects are highly dynamic and can change over time. This makes preservation more difficult, as data can disappear if platforms shut down or stop maintaining archives.

---

## Oldest Digital Library / Archive

One of the earliest and longest-running digital archives related to esports earnings is Esports Earnings, which began operating in the mid-2000s.

https://www.esportsearnings.com/

The site has been maintained through continuous updates as new games and tournaments emerge. Its metadata typically includes game titles, event names, prize pools, dates, and player earnings. While not a formal academic archive, it functions as a historical record for competitive gaming.

---

## Newest Digital Library / Archive

A newer and more collaborative esports archive is Liquipedia.

https://liquipedia.net/

Liquipedia reflects more recent approaches to digital archiving through community-driven editing, standardized templates, and frequent revisions. Compared to older archives, it emphasizes interoperability, transparency, and rapid updates, though it relies heavily on volunteer labor.

---

## Viral Examples

Certain esports tournaments and prize pools have gone viral, especially when events break financial records. Large prize pools often spread rapidly through social media, news coverage, and livestream platforms, drawing attention to esports earnings databases as reference points. Viral moments increase visibility but can also distort representation by focusing attention on a small number of elite games.

---

## Free vs. Proprietary Access

There is a clear divide between free and proprietary access in esports archives. Free platforms like Esports Earnings and Liquipedia provide open access to data, aligning with public digital library ideals discussed by Kahle. In contrast, proprietary platforms such as commercial analytics and statistics sites restrict access through subscriptions.

This divide reflects broader tensions discussed by Besser and Kahle between public knowledge preservation and commercial control over digital cultural records.

---

## Conclusion

Esports earnings and competitive gaming offer a strong case study for understanding born-digital culture, digital archives, and issues of access, preservation, and representation. As a largely online phenomenon, esports highlights both the possibilities and vulnerabilities of digital cultural memory.
